Part 01 Messaging Audit

Zenara's homepage opens with "A New Way to Practice Psychiatry. Built By a Psychiatrist, for Psychiatrists." That's credible. The founder's story, 20 years in practice, a decade of building better workflows from the inside, is genuinely compelling. But the current messaging leans heavily on founder narrative and not enough on the buyer's specific fear: that adopting another clinical technology tool will cost them time they don't have and still not solve the documentation problem.

The audit below is organized by what's working, what isn't, and what's being left on the table. I've looked at the homepage, the Assess product page, case studies, and the resources section. The tabs switch between those four areas.

Hero headline and founder framing Opportunity
What's there now"A New Way to Practice Psychiatry. Built by a Psychiatrist, for Psychiatrists." Followed by a founder's personal letter about what drove him to build it.
The gapThe founder story builds trust but delays the answer to the buyer's first question: what does this actually do for my 4pm patient load? Psychiatrists are time-poor. The hero needs to answer the clinical problem in the first sentence, then earn the right to tell the origin story lower on the page.
"More presence. More freedom. More life." subheadline Vague
What's there nowThe three-phrase subheadline under the main headline feels like a wellness tagline, not a psychiatric workflow tool.
The gapThe psychiatrist buyer is skeptical. "More life" doesn't give them anything to evaluate. "Arrive at your first visit already knowing who's in the room" is the same idea, but concrete enough that a busy clinician can picture it and want it.
Stats: 8-10 diagnoses per patient vs 2.8 benchmark Strong
What's there nowThe clinical validation numbers are buried mid-page. 8-10 diagnoses identified per patient compared to the 2.8 standard of care. 50-75% higher remission rates with measurement-based care.
The fixThese are the numbers that earn immediate attention from a clinician. They need to be above the fold, or at minimum at the very first scroll position, not discovered after reading a founding story. Pull them up. Let the outcome speak first.
CTA: "Get Your Practice Checkup" vs "Start Free" Opportunity
What's there nowTwo CTAs sit side by side: "Start using Assess (free through July 31st)" and "Get Your Practice Checkup." They're pointing at the same audience with different commitment levels but equal prominence.
The fixPrimary CTA for cold traffic should be the free Assess offer, the lowest friction entry point, with a time boundary that creates urgency. "Practice Checkup" is a higher-touch, warmer ask. Make it secondary visually. Run these as an A/B test by traffic source once the campaign machine is running.
Opening positioning: "Most comprehensive mental health assessment" Unclear
What's there now"The most comprehensive mental health assessment." This is a quality claim without a reference point. Comprehensive compared to what? PHQ-9 and GAD-7? Manual intake? The EHR's built-in screener?
The fixName the comparison explicitly. "A 20-minute self-report that surfaces 8-10 clinical findings, versus the 2.8 a standard evaluation typically catches. That gap is what changes treatment trajectory." Concrete, competitive, clinical.
Case studies: David, Miguel, Nadia Strong
What's there nowThree patient stories that show Assess finding things standard screening missed: loneliness misread as dementia, a supplement causing the symptom it was meant to treat, caffeine amplifying anxiety that wasn't anxiety disorder.
OpportunityThese stories are the best marketing asset on the site. They should be the first thing on the Assess page, not a later scroll. Consider a fourth story from a practice management angle: how did the practice owner's time change, not just the patient outcome?
Billing and CPT code section Opportunity
What's there nowThe page lists supported CPT codes (90791, 90792, 96138, etc.) in a bulleted list near the bottom with a compliance disclaimer.
The fixFor a practice owner or practice manager, billable codes aren't a footnote, they're the business case. Turn the billing section into a mini ROI frame: "Assess supports documentation for psychiatric evaluations (90791/90792) and psychological testing codes. Based on typical reimbursement rates and our current clinical validation partnership, a 10-psychiatrist practice sees roughly [X] in captured billing per month." Make the CFO argument the clinician can hand to their billing team.
Texas Center for Lifestyle Medicine reference Opportunity
What's there nowA brief mention that TCLM's partnership with Zenara surfaced bipolar disorder missed for years, undisclosed trauma, and supplements causing the symptoms they were meant to treat.
The fixThis is a named real-world clinical partnership that produced measurable outcomes. It needs its own case study page, not a paragraph on the homepage. A proper case study here (practice size, patient volume, what Assess changed, what the practice said afterward) would be a significant sales enablement asset for any partnership conversation with health systems.
APA 2026 event presence Strong signal
What's there nowZenara has a dedicated APA 2026 landing page. APA Annual Meeting is the single largest gathering of psychiatrists in the US.
OpportunityThe APA presence needs pre-event content (who should come see us and why), an in-event asset (something demos can leave behind beyond a brochure), and a post-event follow-up sequence for leads collected. Three-part event marketing motion, not just a landing page.
Deep-dive guides: CCM vs CoCM vs BHI Strong
What's there nowZenara has produced clinical workflow guides, a CCM vs CoCM vs BHI comparison, an APCM playbook, and a guide for therapy practices adding psychiatric coverage. These are genuinely useful clinical resources.
OpportunityThese guides are organic search gold for practice managers and clinical directors Googling "collaborative care model billing guide" or "how to add psychiatric coverage to therapy practice." They're not promoted anywhere on the homepage. A simple resource hub with email capture on each guide would build a warm list without running paid ads.
ROI Calculator page Underutilized
What's there nowZenara has an ROI Calculator page linked from the footer and resources nav. Good instinct. But it's buried.
The fixThe ROI calculator should be linked directly from every product page and every case study. "See what this looks like for your practice size" is the natural next step after reading a clinical case study. The calculator is doing conversion work if it's findable; right now it's mostly just existing.
Ritual Bell newsletter Opportunity
What's there nowZenara has a newsletter called Ritual Bell listed in the resources nav. The concept (short clinical reads between patients) matches exactly how a psychiatrist consumes content during a day.
OpportunityRitual Bell could be a significant top-of-funnel asset if positioned correctly. "5 minutes of clinical relevance, between patients" is a subscriber pitch. The current landing experience needs an explicit value prop and a clear sample edition. If the content is good, this compounds.
Part 02 GTM Brief: First 90 Days

Zenara has something most early-stage health SaaS companies don't: a founder who practices medicine, clinical validation data, and real case studies with named findings. The GTM problem isn't a product problem, it's a distribution and trust problem. Here's how I'd structure the first 90 days to build both.

The Core Insight

Psychiatrists don't buy software. They adopt what colleagues trust and what doesn't add friction to a day that's already out of control.

The purchasing motion for a clinical tool like Zenara is not a software buying motion. It's a professional trust motion. A psychiatrist will not schedule a demo because they saw a LinkedIn ad. They'll consider it because someone in their peer network mentioned it worked, because they heard the founder speak at APA, or because one of the guides Zenara published answered a billing question they'd been stuck on. The GTM strategy has to be built around earning professional trust, not generating impressions. That means conferences, peer networks, clinical content, and a referral motion, not broad paid channels in month one.

ICP Priority

Who we're going after first

Primary: Independent psychiatry practice owners and group practice medical directors (5-25 clinician practices). They have budget authority, they feel the documentation pain most acutely, and they're evaluating tools without a large procurement process. Secondary: Practice managers and operations leads at behavioral health groups. They're often the ones who bring a tool to the clinician to approve. Not yet: Health systems and enterprise. The sales cycle is too long for the current stage.

Month 1

Build the trust assets the sales team doesn't have yet

Messaging framework. Positioning doc by buyer type. Complete the TCLM case study. Build a proper Assess one-pager that a practice manager can hand to their medical director. Rewrite the homepage hero to lead with clinical outcome, not founder story. Establish the content calendar and define Ritual Bell's voice and frequency. None of this is visible externally yet, but without it, nothing else converts.

Month 2

APA 2026 as the first major demand moment

Pre-event: target list of practices attending, outreach sequence, content drop tied to APA themes (measurement-based care, documentation burden). At event: booth strategy focused on the Assess demo, not the full ZenOS story. Post-event: 48-hour follow-up sequence for every lead. One LinkedIn thought leadership post from the founder during the conference. APA is where the ICP is. It's the highest-leverage demand moment of the year.

Month 3

The content engine that compounds

Launch a structured content calendar: two Ritual Bell editions per month, one long-form guide per month targeting practice management search queries, one case study every 6 weeks. Set up email capture on every guide with a 3-email nurture sequence. Track which content converts to demo requests. The clinical guides Zenara has already written are the foundation. The work is promoting them properly and measuring what actually generates pipeline.

Measurement

How we'll know if it's working

Week 4: messaging framework approved by sales and CEO. Month 2: APA leads entered into CRM with follow-up tracked. Month 3: 3 pieces of content live with traffic and conversion data. Ongoing: cost per demo request by channel, demo-to-trial conversion, trial-to-paid conversion. The channels that produce qualified demos get more budget. The ones that produce impressions but not conversations get cut.

Part 04 Copy Examples

Four pieces across the formats the Zenara marketing function would own most in the first 90 days. Each one is written for a specific job, not to demonstrate range.

Website  ·  Homepage hero rewrite Web copy
Your first visit used to start with forty minutes of fact-finding. Now it starts with medicine.
Zenara's assessment tool does the intake before you walk in. Patient history, symptom patterns, social determinants, medication interactions: organized and surfaced before the first word. So the 50-minute hour is actually spent doing psychiatry, not gathering it.

In clinical validation across 400 patients, Assess identified 8 to 10 meaningful findings per patient. The standard of care catches 2.8. The difference often changes the treatment entirely.
Why this works: The current hero leads with "A New Way to Practice Psychiatry," which is true but doesn't answer the psychiatrist's immediate question: what changes for me tomorrow? This version opens with the specific, felt experience of a first visit eaten by intake, then immediately names what changes and how much. The validation stat at the end earns attention precisely because it comes after a concrete image, not as a standalone claim at the top of the page.
APA 2026  ·  Conference outreach email (psychiatry group practice owner) Email
Subject: One thing we want to show you at APA this year
Hi [Name],

We'll be at APA 2026 and I wanted to reach out ahead of time rather than trying to catch you between sessions.

Zenara is a clinical assessment and care coordination platform built by a practicing psychiatrist. In a partnership with Texas Center for Lifestyle Medicine, our assessment tool identified bipolar disorder that had been missed for years, caught undisclosed trauma, and flagged a supplement causing the symptom it was supposed to treat. Across 400 patients in clinical validation, it surfaces 8-10 meaningful findings per assessment compared to the 2.8 typically caught in standard evaluations.

We're not a documentation shortcut or another EHR plugin. The system is designed to extend what a psychiatrist can see before the visit even starts, so the 50-minute hour is actually spent on medicine.

If you're at APA and this sounds worth 15 minutes, I'd love to show you a live assessment run. Would Tuesday afternoon work?
Why this works: Conference outreach emails fail because they lead with "excited to connect" and then describe a product in marketing language. This version acknowledges the context (APA, busy), leads with a specific real-world finding that a clinician would find genuinely alarming, backs it with a concrete stat, then draws a clear distinction from the two things a psychiatrist is most tired of: documentation tools and EHR plugins. The ask is specific and low-friction. "Tuesday afternoon" is more human than "a time that works for you."
Sales Enablement  ·  Objection card (when they say "we already use an EHR with intake forms") Enablement
When the prospect says: "Our EHR already handles intake."
Don't counter the EHR. Ask what the intake actually surfaces.

"That makes sense, and Zenara isn't replacing your EHR. The question worth asking is: what does your current intake actually tell you before you walk in? Most EHR intake forms collect demographics and a chief complaint. They don't surface supplement interactions, social isolation patterns, PHQ-9 trends across visits, or the kind of findings that change a diagnosis. In our clinical validation, standard assessments caught an average of 2.8 meaningful findings per patient. Zenara's assessment caught 8 to 10. That gap is where treatment trajectories get changed, or missed. I can show you what a completed Zenara assessment looks like for a real patient profile in about 10 minutes. Want to see what it actually pulls?"
Why this works: The instinct is to defend against the EHR comparison by listing features. That positions Zenara as an EHR competitor, which loses the frame entirely. This card reframes the conversation around what intake actually produces, clinically, and then uses the 2.8 vs 8-10 stat to make the gap concrete rather than argued. The offer at the end ("see what it actually pulls") shifts from debate to demonstration, which is where the product wins.
Content  ·  Ritual Bell edition (clinician audience, between patients) Newsletter
The supplement that was causing the symptom it was supposed to treat.
Miguel was 18. PHQ-9 of 4. GAD-7 of 2. By every standard screen, he was fine.

He wasn't fine. He had debilitating symptoms that came and went, completely invisible to tools that average across time. What Zenara's assessment found: a supplement he was taking for brain fog had brain fog as a documented side effect. The treatment was causing the problem.

Standard intake didn't ask about supplements in enough depth to catch it. A rushed first visit might have started him on an SSRI he didn't need.

We built Assess to find what standard care misses. Not because clinicians aren't looking, but because the tools we hand them weren't designed to look at the whole picture. This is one case. There are hundreds more like it in every practice.

If you've got a patient whose presentation doesn't quite add up, we'd be happy to run a sample Assess and show you what it surfaces. No sales pitch, just the output.
Why this works: Ritual Bell should read the way a good clinical colleague talks, not the way a SaaS company blogs. This edition leads with a specific, surprising patient story that a psychiatrist will recognize the shape of from their own practice. The product is present but secondary to the clinical insight. The CTA at the end ("no sales pitch, just the output") reduces friction by naming and dismissing the thing that makes a clinician hesitate to engage with a vendor.
